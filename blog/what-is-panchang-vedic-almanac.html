What Is Panchang? A Guide to the Vedic Almanac

By Astrologer Ananya 10 min read Vedic Astrology · Beginner

Panchang is the Vedic almanac — a daily timetable of the sky whose name means "five limbs." Those five limbs (Tithi, Vara, Nakshatra, Yoga, and Karana) encode the positions of the Sun and Moon into the working elements of Hindu timekeeping, and together they are used to fix festivals, fasts, and the auspicious moments known as Muhurta.

If a birth Kundli is the sky frozen at one person's birth, the Panchang is the sky organised for everyone, day by day. It is the reference an astrologer, a priest, or a family opens to answer everyday questions: When is the festival this year? Which day is good for the housewarming? When should the wedding rituals begin? This guide unpacks its five limbs and how they are read.

What "Panchang" Means

The word comes from Sanskrit: pancha ("five") and anga ("limbs" or parts). A Panchang (also spelled Panchangam) is therefore a "five-limbed" almanac. Each limb is a different way of measuring a day derived from the ever-changing angle between the Sun and the Moon — so the Panchang is really the Sun–Moon relationship translated into five practical readings.

Crucially, the Panchang is calculated for a specific place. Because it is tied to local sunrise, the same calendar day can carry slightly different values in different cities, which is why an accurate Panchang always asks for your location.

1. Tithi — the Lunar Day

A tithi is a lunar day, defined by the angle between the Sun and Moon: each tithi completes when the Moon moves 12 degrees ahead of the Sun. Thirty tithis make one lunar month, split into two fortnights (paksha) — the bright, waxing half (Shukla Paksha) leading to the full moon, and the dark, waning half (Krishna Paksha) leading to the new moon. Tithis anchor most festivals and fasts; Ekadashi fasting and full-moon (Purnima) observances are tithi-based.

2. Vara — the Weekday

Vara is simply the weekday, measured from one sunrise to the next, and each is ruled by a graha: Sunday by the Sun, Monday by the Moon, Tuesday by Mars, Wednesday by Mercury, Thursday by Jupiter, Friday by Venus, Saturday by Saturn. The ruling planet lends the day its character, which is why particular tasks and worship are traditionally matched to particular weekdays.

3. Nakshatra — the Lunar Mansion

This is the nakshatra the Moon occupies that day — one of the 27 lunar mansions the Moon travels through, spending roughly a day in each. The day's nakshatra sets its emotional tone and is heavily weighed for marriage muhurtas, travel, naming ceremonies, and other beginnings.

4. Yoga — the Sun–Moon Combination

Yoga here is a technical almanac term (not the physical practice). It is formed by adding the longitudes of the Sun and Moon, producing one of 27 yogas. Each yoga describes the subtle quality of the day — some are considered favourable, others cautionary — and it is consulted as a supporting factor when judging timing.

5. Karana — the Half-Tithi

A karana is half of a tithi, so each tithi contains two karanas. There are 11 karanas in total (some fixed, some recurring), and they give a finer, sub-daily measure of time. Karanas are especially used in muhurta selection to pin down practical windows for transactions, ceremonies, and action within a day.

Why the Panchang Matters

The Panchang is not just a calendar to look at; it is a tool to act on. Its main uses include:

  • Muhurta — choosing an auspicious time to begin something important: a wedding, housewarming (griha pravesh), business launch, or journey. A good muhurta is one where the five limbs align favourably.
  • Festivals and fasts — Diwali, Holi, Navratri, Ekadashi and countless observances fall on specific tithis and nakshatras, which the Panchang fixes each year.
  • Daily inauspicious windows — periods such as Rahu Kalam, Yamaganda, and Gulika Kalam are listed so they can be avoided for new undertakings.
  • Sunrise, sunset, and moon timings — the astronomical backbone the rest of the day's calculations rest on.
How a muhurta is chosen: an astrologer looks for a moment when the tithi, weekday, nakshatra, yoga, and karana are all suitable for the task at hand — and, ideally, when it also agrees with the individual's own birth chart. It is the five limbs working in concert, not any single one, that makes a time "auspicious."

A Note on Regional Differences

There is no single universal Panchang. Traditions differ, chiefly in how the lunar month is reckoned: the Amanta system (common in western and southern India) ends the month at the new moon, while the Purnimanta system (common in the north) ends it at the full moon. Regional almanacs — and different ayanamsa settings — can therefore list a festival on slightly different dates. This is not error but tradition; if precision matters for a ritual, follow the Panchang of your own regional or family custom.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Panchang the same as a horoscope?

No. A horoscope (Kundli) is cast for one person's birth. The Panchang is a general daily almanac of the sky for a given place, used for timing festivals, fasts, and auspicious moments — not for reading an individual's life.

Why does my Panchang differ from someone else's for the same day?

Two reasons: location (the Panchang is tied to local sunrise) and tradition (the Amanta vs Purnimanta month systems and different ayanamsa settings). Both can shift tithi timings and festival dates slightly between regions.

What is a Muhurta?

A Muhurta is an auspicious window of time chosen for an important activity. It is selected by finding a moment when the Panchang's five limbs are favourable for the task — and ideally supportive of the person's own birth chart as well.

Do I need to consult the Panchang daily?

For everyday life, most people check it mainly around festivals, fasts, and important new beginnings. Devout households and priests consult it more regularly, and astrologers use it whenever they select a muhurta.
