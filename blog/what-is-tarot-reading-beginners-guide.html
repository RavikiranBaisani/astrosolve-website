What Is Tarot Reading? A Beginner's Guide

By Astrologer Ananya 8 min read Tarot · Beginner

Tarot is a deck of 78 illustrated cards that people use to reflect on a question, a mood, or a moment in their life. A reading lays out a small number of cards in a pattern called a spread, and their images become prompts for thinking something through rather than a fixed prediction of what will happen. It is a practice worth understanding on its own terms, separate from Vedic astrology, even though many people explore both.

I get asked about tarot often enough, usually by someone who has had a reading at a fair or seen one on social media and wants to know if there's anything real behind it. The honest answer is that tarot is a structured tool for reflection built around a deck of symbolic images, and its value depends heavily on how it's used. This guide walks through what the deck actually contains, where the practice came from, how a basic reading is done, and how a beginner can try it without over-promising what it can deliver — much like the approach I'd recommend for anyone starting out with simple daily astrological remedies.

What Tarot Actually Is

A tarot deck has 78 cards, each carrying a picture and a set of associations that readers draw on to interpret it. During a reading, a person shuffles the deck while holding a question or situation in mind, then draws one or more cards. The images and their traditional meanings are used as prompts, and the reader (or the person reading for themselves) interprets how those prompts might relate to the situation at hand.

What tarot is not is a guaranteed window into the future. Reputable readers, and most serious writing on the subject, treat it as a tool for self-reflection and for exploring a question from angles you might not have considered — not as a mechanism that produces certain outcomes.

Tarot vs. fortune-telling: A tarot reading describes possibilities and perspectives connected to a question, shaped by how the cards are interpreted in the moment. It is not a claim that a specific event is locked in and will happen regardless of what you do. Treat any reading — tarot or otherwise — that promises a certain, unavoidable outcome with healthy skepticism.

A Brief, Honest History

It's tempting to want tarot to be ancient, but the honest history is more modest than the mystical origin stories that circulate online. Tarot cards began in 15th-century Italy as a set of playing cards used for a trick-taking card game, similar in spirit to bridge, with the earliest known decks appearing among elite Italian families. The extra set of illustrated trump cards that later became the Major Arcana was part of the game itself, not a divination tool.

The deck traveled to France over the following centuries, and by the 17th and 18th centuries a standardized version known as the Tarot de Marseille had become common, produced by card makers in and around that city. It was only in the late 18th century that tarot's meaning began to shift: French writers and occultists started speculating that the cards held hidden esoteric wisdom, with some claiming (without real evidence) links to ancient Egypt. That reinterpretation, continued and expanded by occult and esoteric circles through the 19th century, is where tarot's use as a divination and reflection tool actually comes from.

So tarot's documented history as a reading practice is a few centuries old at most — younger than many assume, and considerably younger than the textual traditions Vedic astrology draws on. That doesn't make it less useful as a reflective tool; it just means the "ancient mystical origins" framing you sometimes see is more marketing than history.

How the Deck Is Structured

The 78 cards split into two groups, and understanding this split is the fastest way to make sense of any spread.

Major Arcana — 22 Cards

The Major Arcana are the 22 "trump" cards, each with its own name and image — The Fool, The Magician, The Tower, The Star, and so on. These are generally read as pointing to bigger themes: significant turning points, life lessons, or forces that feel larger than a day-to-day decision. When a Major Arcana card turns up in a reading, it's often taken as a sign that the question at hand touches something more substantial than routine.

Minor Arcana — 56 Cards

The Minor Arcana work more like a standard card deck, split into four suits of 14 cards each (Ace through 10, plus four court cards). These are generally read as reflecting everyday matters — the practical texture of work, relationships, money, and daily choices, rather than sweeping life themes.

These associations aren't universal law — different schools and individual authors describe the suits with somewhat different emphasis — but the four-element framework above is the one most commonly taught to beginners and is a reasonable starting point.

How a Basic Reading Works

A reading is less mysterious in practice than it sounds. The steps are simple, and beginners tend to overthink them:

  1. Frame a question or focus. This can be a specific question ("what should I consider about this job change?") or simply an open focus for the day.
  2. Shuffle. The person shuffles the deck by hand while holding the question loosely in mind, stopping whenever it feels natural.
  3. Draw cards according to a spread. A spread is just a pattern that assigns meaning to each position. A single card drawn for the day is the simplest spread there is.
  4. Interpret. Each card's traditional meaning is considered alongside its position in the spread and, often, its relationship to neighboring cards.

A widely used beginner spread is the three-card past–present–future layout: the first card drawn is read as reflecting something already in motion, the second as the present situation, and the third as where things may be heading if nothing changes. It's a compact way to structure a reflection without needing to learn dozens of card combinations at once.

What Tarot Is Actually Good For

Used sensibly, tarot works best as a prompt for thinking, not a substitute for it. People commonly use it to:

  • Sit with a question they've been avoiding and look at it from a fresh angle
  • Structure a reflective moment, similar to journaling with a visual prompt
  • Check in with themselves on a recurring basis, such as a daily or weekly draw
  • Get a second perspective on a decision from a reader trained in the imagery

What it should not be used for is replacing considered judgment on medical, legal, or financial matters, or as a guarantee that a specific event will or won't occur. A good reading raises questions and possibilities worth thinking about; it doesn't hand down verdicts.

How a Beginner Can Start

You don't need to memorize all 78 cards before doing your first reading. A practical starting point:

  • Get one deck. The Rider-Waite-Smith deck is the most commonly recommended starting deck because its imagery is widely documented and most guidebooks reference it.
  • Start with a daily single-card draw. Pull one card each morning and spend a few minutes considering how its themes might relate to your day, rather than treating it as an instruction.
  • Keep a journal. Note the card, your interpretation, and how the day actually unfolded. Over weeks, this builds your own working familiarity with the cards far faster than memorizing meanings from a book.
  • Move to small spreads gradually. Once daily draws feel comfortable, try the three-card past–present–future spread on a real question.

Consistency matters more than complexity when you're starting out. A short, honest daily habit will teach you more about how you relate to the cards than an elaborate ten-card spread attempted once and abandoned.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can tarot predict my future?

Treat it as a tool for reflection and exploring possibilities connected to a question, not as a guarantee of what will happen. Interpretations vary by reader and by tradition, and outcomes depend on real-world choices and circumstances, not the cards alone.

Is tarot part of Vedic astrology?

No. Tarot developed separately in Europe and sits outside the Vedic astrology tradition, which rests on its own distinct texts and calculation methods involving planets, houses, and nakshatras — the same birth-chart framework covered in our guide to what a Kundli is. Some people enjoy both as complementary reflective practices, but they are not the same system and shouldn't be conflated.

Do I need special abilities to read tarot?

No. Reading tarot is a learnable skill built on familiarity with the cards' traditional meanings and practice interpreting them in context, similar to learning any symbolic system. Some readers describe an intuitive element to it, but consistent practice is what most beginners actually need.

How is a three-card spread read?

The three cards are typically assigned to past, present, and future in that order. The first card reflects something already in motion, the second the current situation, and the third a likely direction if things continue as they are — not a fixed outcome.

Tarot rewards practice, but a session with someone experienced can help you see a spread more clearly, especially when you're starting out.
