Major Arcana Cards Explained: Meanings and Symbolism

By Astrologer Ananya 10 min read Tarot · Beginner

The 22 Major Arcana cards are tarot's headline cast — the cards that show up when a reading touches something big: a turning point, a lesson, a fear worth naming. Read in order, they trace a single story, often called the Fool's Journey, from wide-eyed beginning to hard-won wholeness. Understanding that story makes the individual cards far easier to hold onto than memorising 22 unrelated meanings.

A tarot deck has 78 cards, and only 22 of them are Major Arcana. The other 56, the Minor Arcana, are split into four suits and read much like a calendar of daily weather — useful, specific, changeable. The Major Arcana is closer to climate: it points at the larger conditions a life is moving through. This piece is a reference guide to those 22 cards — what they represent as a set, a readable walk through their sequence, and a note on why they tend to dominate a reading whenever one turns up.

What the Major Arcana Represents

Each Major Arcana card is numbered, 0 through 21, and stands for an archetype — a role or force broad enough to appear in nearly every human life: the innocent beginner, the ruler, the lover, the crisis that ends one chapter and forces the next. Because they are archetypes rather than everyday situations, Major Arcana cards tend to describe themes that unfold over months or years, not the outcome of tomorrow's phone call.

Read in their numbered order, the 22 cards are conventionally described as the Fool's Journey: The Fool (0) is the figure setting out, and the following 21 cards are the encounters, tests, and thresholds that shape their path toward The World (XXI), a card of completion. It is a helpful narrative scaffold rather than a fixed doctrine — different tarot schools frame the journey's stages somewhat differently, but the arc from naive beginning to earned wholeness is broadly shared across traditions.

The Fool's Journey, Grouped by Theme

Working through all 22 cards one by one gets tedious fast. It reads better grouped by what stage of the journey they belong to.

Beginnings: The Fool, The Magician, The High Priestess

The Fool (0) is potential before it has taken shape — a leap of faith, a fresh start, openness that hasn't yet met consequence. The Magician (I) follows with the tools already in hand: will, skill, and the resources needed to act, provided they're pointed with intention rather than scattered. The High Priestess (II) is the counterweight to that action — intuition, withheld knowledge, the sense that something true is forming beneath the surface before it's ready to be spoken. Together these three set up the whole deck's central tension between doing and knowing.

Foundation and Structure: The Empress, The Emperor, The Hierophant, The Lovers

The Empress (III) brings abundance, nurture, and creative growth into the picture. The Emperor (IV) answers with structure, authority, and boundaries — the discipline that lets growth hold its shape. The Hierophant (V) represents tradition, institutions, and inherited belief, while The Lovers (VI) is less about romance specifically than about a meaningful choice, usually one that tests values against attraction. This stretch of cards is where the Fool starts encountering the world's existing systems and has to decide what to accept and what to question.

Momentum and Will: The Chariot, Strength, The Hermit, Wheel of Fortune

The Chariot (VII) is drive and controlled willpower — opposing forces held together and pointed in one direction. Strength is quieter power, patience and compassion overcoming brute force. The Hermit (IX) is a deliberate withdrawal, seeking answers alone before returning with something to share. Wheel of Fortune (X) marks a shift the person didn't fully choose — luck, cycles, and the reminder that circumstances turn regardless of how prepared anyone feels.

A numbering quirk worth knowing: Strength and Justice don't sit at the same numbers in every deck. Older Marseille-style decks place Justice at VIII and Strength at XI; the widely used Rider-Waite-Smith deck swapped them, putting Strength at VIII and Justice at XI, for reasons tied to the astrological correspondences its designers favoured. Neither placement is "correct" — it depends which deck lineage a reader learned from.

Growth Through Challenge: Justice, The Hanged Man, Death, Temperance

Justice is cause and effect made visible — fairness, truth, and a reckoning that's been earned rather than imposed. The Hanged Man (XII) asks for a pause, a change in perspective that only comes from surrender rather than more effort. Death (XIII) is the deck's most misread card: it almost never means literal death in a reading, but a definitive ending that clears the way for something else to begin. Temperance (XIV) follows as the integration step — blending, patience, and moderation after the upheaval.

Confronting Shadow: The Devil, The Tower

The Devil (XV) names what has quietly become a trap — addiction, unhealthy attachment, or a self-imposed limit mistaken for a wall with no door. The Tower (XVI) is sudden, external collapse: a structure or belief that was already unstable being knocked down all at once. Uncomfortable as they are, both cards tend to appear right before real change becomes possible, not instead of it.

Culmination: The Star, The Moon, The Sun, Judgement, The World

The Star (XVII) arrives after the Tower's wreckage as quiet hope and renewed faith. The Moon (XVIII) is the fog before full clarity — uncertainty, illusion, and the instruction to trust instinct when facts are incomplete. The Sun (XIX) is the clearest card in the deck: vitality, success, and things working out plainly and well. Judgement (XX) is a reckoning and a calling — a moment of reflection that leads to a genuine rebirth or renewed purpose. The World (XXI) closes the sequence: completion, wholeness, and a cycle finished on its own terms — after which, tellingly, the Fool's number is 0 again, ready to begin a new cycle.

Upright and Reversed: A Brief Note

Most modern tarot practice reads a card differently depending on whether it lands upright or upside down (reversed) in a spread. Upright, a card generally shows its meaning in fairly direct, outward form. Reversed, the same archetype is often read as blocked, internalised, delayed, or expressed in its more shadow-side form — The Sun upright might point to straightforward success, while The Sun reversed might suggest that same joy is being withheld or not yet recognised.

Reversals aren't universal practice. Some readers and traditions, particularly those closer to the older Marseille style, don't use reversed meanings at all and instead read every card upright, leaning more heavily on its position in the spread and its neighbouring cards. Neither approach is more "correct" — it's a matter of which method a reader was trained in and finds genuinely useful.

Why the Major Arcana Carries More Weight in a Reading

When a spread is dominated by Minor Arcana cards, a reading tends to describe the texture of daily circumstances — a conversation, a short-term worry, a passing mood. When Major Arcana cards cluster in a spread, particularly cards like Death, The Tower, or The World, the reading is usually pointing at something structural: a genuine turning point, a lesson that recurs until it's learned, or a chapter of life ending rather than a single event passing. That's the practical reason experienced readers pay closer attention the moment a Major Arcana card appears — it tends to mark the difference between an incident and a theme.

None of this makes tarot a forecasting tool with guaranteed outcomes. A Major Arcana card names a pattern or a crossroads worth paying attention to; it doesn't fix a date or promise a result. It's best treated as a structured way to reflect on where you actually stand, not a substitute for your own judgment on decisions that matter.

Tarot and Vedic Astrology: Related, Not the Same

It's worth being upfront that tarot sits outside the Vedic astrology tradition. Vedic astrology (Jyotisha) is built on planetary positions, houses, and dashas calculated from a birth chart — a system with its own long textual lineage. Tarot developed separately, out of European card games and later esoteric traditions, and its symbolism doesn't map onto the grahas or a Kundli in any formal way. AstroSolve treats tarot as a complementary reflective practice rather than an extension of Vedic astrology — useful in its own right, but answering a different kind of question than a birth chart does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to memorise all 22 Major Arcana cards to start reading tarot?

No. Most beginners find it easier to learn the Fool's Journey as a story first, then fill in individual card detail over time. Grouping the cards thematically, as above, is usually a faster way in than memorising 22 isolated meanings.

Does pulling Death always mean something bad is coming?

Not literally, and rarely something bad. Death in tarot almost always represents an ending that clears space for a new phase, not a literal event. Context from the surrounding cards usually makes clear what exactly is ending.

Why do some decks number Strength and Justice differently?

It comes down to lineage. Traditional Marseille-style decks place Justice at VIII and Strength at XI; the Rider-Waite-Smith deck swapped the two numbers. Both are established conventions, so it's worth knowing which one your deck follows.

Is tarot part of Vedic astrology?

No. They're separate traditions with separate origins and methods. Vedic astrology works from a birth chart and planetary calculations; tarot works from symbolic cards and their arrangement in a spread. AstroSolve offers both as distinct tools rather than treating one as a substitute for the other.
