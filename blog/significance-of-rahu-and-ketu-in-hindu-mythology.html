Significance of Rahu and Ketu in Hindu Mythology

By Astrologer Ananya 9 min read Hindu Mythology · Beginner

Before Rahu and Ketu were ever plotted on a birth chart, they were characters in a story — one Asura, split in two, forever chasing and forever fleeing the Sun and Moon that gave him away. Understanding that story is the fastest way to understand why these two "shadow planets" behave the way they do in astrology, and why they are always found exactly opposite one another, wherever they fall.

Ask most people what Rahu and Ketu "mean" and you'll usually get a chart-reading answer — which house they sit in, which planet they're troubling, what remedy fixes it. That's useful, practical information, and we cover it in depth in our guide to Rahu-Ketu Dosha. But it skips the origin entirely. Rahu and Ketu aren't abstract mathematical points that astrologers decided to track for convenience — they're named after, and modeled on, a single mythological figure whose fate is one of the most vivid stories in the Puranas. This article tells that story: the churning of the cosmic ocean, the theft of a few drops of nectar, and the beheading that created two opposite points in the sky.

Samudra Manthan: The Churning of the Ocean of Milk

The story begins with a problem the Devas (gods) couldn't solve alone. Weakened by a sage's curse and outmatched by the Asuras (demons), the Devas approached Vishnu for a way to reclaim their strength and immortality. His answer was Samudra Manthan — the churning of the Kshira Sagara, the Ocean of Milk, to draw out Amrita, the nectar of immortality, along with a host of other treasures hidden within it.

Churning an entire ocean required scale the Devas didn't have on their own, so they struck an uneasy truce with the Asuras: both sides would churn together and share what emerged. Mount Mandara was uprooted and used as the churning rod, and the great serpent Vasuki agreed to be wound around it as the churning rope — Devas pulling one end, Asuras the other, back and forth, for what the texts describe as an immense, exhausting span of time. Vishnu himself, in his Kurma (tortoise) avatar, is said to have supported the mountain on his back so it wouldn't sink into the ocean floor as the churning began.

Treasure after treasure rose from the churned ocean — among them the physician-god Dhanvantari, finally bearing a pot of Amrita. And that's when the truce began to fall apart.

Svarbhanu's Disguise

The moment the nectar appeared, both sides wanted it entirely for themselves, and a scramble broke out. Vishnu, to keep the peace and make sure the nectar went only to the Devas, took the form of Mohini — a beautiful, enchanting woman — and offered to distribute the Amrita fairly, seating the two groups in separate rows and moving down the line pouring nectar into each god's cup.

One Asura, Svarbhanu, saw his chance. He slipped out of the Asura row, disguised himself as a Deva, and quietly took a seat among the gods. Mohini, moving down the row, poured nectar for him too — and it very nearly reached his throat.

It didn't. Surya (the Sun) and Chandra (the Moon), seated nearby, recognized Svarbhanu despite the disguise and alerted Vishnu at once. In that same instant, Vishnu's Sudarshan Chakra — his discus — flew and severed Svarbhanu's head from his body, right at the neck, exactly where the nectar had reached.

But the nectar had already touched his throat. A drop of Amrita, once tasted, cannot be undone — and so, even beheaded, Svarbhanu did not die. Both halves of him lived on as two separate, immortal beings: the head became Rahu, and the body — headless, tailed like a serpent in most depictions — became Ketu.

Why this explains the astronomy: in astrology, Rahu and Ketu are not physical planets but the two points where the Moon's orbital path crosses the ecliptic (the Sun's apparent path) — the North Node and South Node respectively. Because they come from a single being cut in half, the myth gives a poetic reason for a real geometric fact: Rahu and Ketu are always exactly 180 degrees apart in any chart, one always directly opposite the other, never anywhere else.

A Grudge That Explains Eclipses

Svarbhanu's story doesn't end with the beheading. Because it was Surya and Chandra who exposed him before the nectar could fully protect him, tradition holds that Rahu carries a lasting grudge against the Sun and Moon. Periodically, the story goes, Rahu catches up to one of them and swallows it whole — and for a few moments, the Sun or Moon vanishes from the sky. But because Rahu has no throat or body left to hold anything, the swallowed light simply passes through and reappears on the other side. This is the traditional mythological account of solar and lunar eclipses: not destruction, but a brief, ongoing act of an old grudge playing out in the sky, again and again, forever unresolved.

Myth and mechanism, kept separate: it's worth being direct about this. Eclipses are a fully explained astronomical event — a solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Earth and Sun, and a lunar eclipse when the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, both only possible near the lunar nodes. The Rahu-swallowing story is a rich piece of devotional tradition that explains why eclipses cluster near the nodes in cultural, symbolic terms — it isn't a rival physical explanation, and it isn't meant to be read as one. Holding both truths at once, without asking one to disprove the other, is exactly the spirit in which this legend has been passed down for generations.

The Head and the Tail: Why Rahu and Ketu Feel So Different

Because Rahu and Ketu come from the same being, it's tempting to think they'd carry the same nature. Astrologically, they're read as near opposites — and the myth explains why.

Rahu is the head. It was the part of Svarbhanu driven enough to disguise himself among the gods and reach for something not meant for him. That hunger is exactly what Rahu is said to represent in a chart: desire, ambition, illusion (Maya), worldly obsession, and a pull toward the unconventional or the foreign — things outside one's own natural territory. Rahu wants, magnifies, and rarely feels finished wanting.

Ketu is the body. Headless, it has no mouth to crave with and no mind left to scheme with — only the memory of having once been whole. That absence is exactly what Ketu is said to represent: detachment, spirituality, liberation (Moksha), and an orientation toward the past rather than the future. Where Rahu reaches outward, Ketu turns inward; some traditions describe Ketu as inherently more spiritually inclined than Rahu, despite having come from precisely the same figure, precisely because it lost the part of itself capable of worldly craving.

From Myth to Chart: How This Story Lives On in Astrology

The Samudra Manthan legend isn't just a colorful backstory — it's the reason Rahu and Ketu are classed as Chhaya Graha, shadow planets, distinct from the seven visible, physical grahas. They own no zodiac sign of their own, cast no light, and are read purely through the qualities the myth assigns them: reaching and releasing, craving and letting go. When astrologers talk about a "Rahu-Ketu axis" in a chart, they are, in a real sense, still talking about Svarbhanu — one being, permanently split, always found on opposite sides of the same line.

This mythological picture is the foundation for how the nodes are actually read in a birth chart — which houses they occupy, which planets they afflict, and what that means for a person's life. There's also a well-known related configuration worth knowing about: Kaal Sarp Dosha, which is said to form when every other planet in a chart falls hemmed between Rahu and Ketu on the same side of their axis — effectively caught inside the serpent's coils. Frequently Asked Questions

Are Rahu and Ketu really the same being?

Yes, according to the legend. Both emerge from a single Asura, Svarbhanu, whose head became Rahu and whose headless body became Ketu after Vishnu's Sudarshan Chakra severed him. This shared origin is the traditional explanation for why the two are always found exactly opposite each other in a birth chart — they are read as one entity split into two halves.

Do Rahu and Ketu actually cause eclipses?

Not physically. Eclipses are a well-understood astronomical event caused by the Moon or Earth blocking sunlight at specific orbital alignments. The story of Rahu "swallowing" the Sun or Moon is a traditional, symbolic explanation for why eclipses happen only near the lunar nodes — it is cultural and devotional meaning layered onto a real astronomical mechanism, not a claim about physical causation.

Why is Rahu linked to desire and Ketu to detachment?

The symbolism follows directly from the myth. Rahu is the head — the part of Svarbhanu that wanted the nectar of immortality badly enough to disguise himself among the gods — so it is associated with craving, ambition, and worldly pursuit. Ketu is the headless body, with no mouth or mind left to desire anything, so it is associated with detachment, introspection, and spiritual release.

How does this myth relate to Rahu-Ketu Dosha and Kaal Sarp Dosha?
